**THE INTRODUCTION**

"For those who don't know me yet...

I am an African.

From Kenya.

Born to the Luo community in Nyakach.

My mother called me Reuben Oduk, while destiny blessed me with the courtesy name Jowikage.

Hamjambo, na karibuni sana katika pre-launch of The Great African Grid initiative."

*[Pause. Let it settle.]*

Allow me to tell a story I have always carried with me.

A young woman called Ajawo Kwasakwasa and her son called Jowi Kwasakwasa lived in a desert where only corn grew at a far distant oasis. Her family had nothing — no food, no education, no future that anyone could see. But she had ambition.

She decided to sell popcorn. Ajawo began harvesting corn seeds at a distant oasis many miles away. The corn was surrounded by poisonous thorns that made it tricky to harvest. She never gave up on the idea anyway. She stood by the road. She held up her tray to every passing car. Every day. Hours passed. Cars of different models and years came and went. Dust rose around her and her son. The sun burned her neck. Her arms grew tired. But she stayed.

No one bought the popcorn. Ajawo's health was drastically declining, since she was pricked by the poisonous thorns when getting the corn and bitten by insects along the way to the oasis.

Then one day, two cars stopped. They seemed to be traveling together. The young woman and her son rushed over, hoping that that was their chance. She went to the first car. It was a completely different model from the second car. She reached the driver's side window to speak. Her son, who was more curious about what was inside, could not help gazing inside the car. At the back seat were the driver's son and daughter sat. They had a lot of items that the poor boy could not believe existed in the world.

The female driver looked at the popcorn, then the son. The wrappers looked old, like they could tear open and the popcorn would fall out. To show she cared, the female driver gave Ajawo one coin — but did not take the popcorn. Then she drove away.

The two went to the second car, which was a different model from the first, another female driver with her family. The same thing happened. Another coin. More interesting items inside the car.

Ajawo then had two coins. But in the desert, two coins were never enough to survive with a family. They could only buy half a loaf of dry bread.

That night, Jowi Kwasakwasa sat alone thinking how the different car models looked amazing and efficient in their own ways. The kids in both cars had items of all kinds. The piece of bread Jowi had was dry. His throat was parched. He needed water. And as he sat there feeling thirsty, lost in a sea of imagination, an idea struck through his mind.

He remembered seeing empty water bottles in both cars. Not given to him. Just left behind. So he made the long trip to the oasis. The journey was never easy. The oasis was miles away, and attacks were prone from poisonous desert insects.

The following month, Ajawo and her son Jowi did not stand by the road holding popcorn. They set up a small table under a shade. They had filled several empty water bottles they collected with cold water, given the low temperature at night. And began selling water.

The same people who had passed the popcorn stopped for the water. They bought. They drank. They bought again.

Why? Because they were in the middle of a desert. And in a desert, no one needs popcorn. They need water.

The business became successful such that Ajawo Kwasakwasa and her son Jowi Kwasakwasa began opening new stop points and channeling the water from the then-renovated oasis to the new water stops. Every water stop point slowly became towns and business centers as people began investing and living around the water points Ajawo and her son had set up across the desert.

Ladies and gentlemen, the son did not change his goal. He changed his plan. That young woman is Africa, our motherland. That boy is us Africans. For too long, we have used internet,seminars,meetings, conferences , holding up the word Third World as a badge to receive aid for survival. It's time we stop selling popcorn. We start selling water.

**PART TWO: WHAT AFRICA WAS**

But before I tell you about the water, let me take you back. Way back. Because Ajawo and her son were not born in a desert.

Before the world knew of great libraries, there was Timbuktu. A city where books were worth more than gold. Where universities stood and scholars gathered from across the known world to study mathematics, astronomy, medicine, and law.

Before the current history knew of billionaires, there was Mansa Musa, who had immeasurable wealth.

Before the world knew of mobile money, innovative mindsets from Africa had already set stage for the future of the current mobile payment systems used globally.

Before the world knew of great kingdoms, there were the empires of Ghana, Mali, and Songhai — vast civilizations built on trade, not conquest. There were the city-states of the Swahili coast, where merchants sailed as traders, not as captives.

Before the world knew of advanced architecture, we had pyramids in Egypt and Sudan, now tourist attraction sites because of physics and architecture.

Before the world knew of community philosophy, there was Ubuntu — "I am because we are." A philosophy of shared humanity. A philosophy that said: a person is a person through other people.

We were not always thirsty. We had wells. We had rivers. We had civilizations that lasted longer than many that came after.

Then the wagons came. First the slave ships. Then the colonizers. Then the borders drawn with rulers across maps where no African had ever been consulted. Then the extraction, including our dignity. Much was taken. Little was given back.

And when the flags came down, something was left behind. Something we have been carrying ever since.

**PART THREE: THE THIRD WORLD WRAPPER**

After independence, everyone including Africans looked at Africa and saw two words. One label. Third World.

And inside that wrapper was poverty. Not the poverty of resources — we have always been rich beneath the soil. The poverty of systems.

Hunger. Not the hunger of land — we have the most arable land on the continent. The hunger of logistics and better economies. The hunger that comes when food rots in one village while children go to sleep hungry in the next.

Corruption. Not the corruption of character — our people are among the most honest on earth. The corruption of impunity. The corruption that comes when there are no consequences.

Ethnic division. Not the hatred of neighbor — we have lived together for centuries. The division that comes when lie is used as a tool of control.

Low life expectancy. Low education. Low expectations. Unemployment.

A continent measured not by what it has, but by what it lacks.

This is what we have been selling.

"Africa is poor. Africa is hungry. Africa is corrupt. Africa needs help."

Philanthropists and developed countries stepped in. They gave a little. They loaned a little. They sent experts. The experts wrote reports. The problems remained while the rest of the world drove past us in all aspects, from education to living standards.

Because no one buys poverty. No one invests in hunger. No one puts their money into a wrapper labeled "Third World" and expects a return.
